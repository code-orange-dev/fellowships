# Facilitation Playbook

**How to run a session that works when you are not the smartest person in the room.**

Written for Educator Fellows. You do not need to be a deep technical expert to run Code Orange sessions well - you need to be a curious Bitcoiner who prepares properly and is honest about the edges of what you know.

---

## The core idea

**Your job is not to be the expert. Your job is to make the room think.**

The best sessions we have run were not the ones with the most knowledgeable facilitator. They were the ones where the facilitator asked good questions, kept everyone moving, and was comfortable saying "I do not know - let us find out."

A facilitator who knows one week more than the room is often *better* than a world expert, because they remember what confusion feels like.

---

## Before the session

**Do the exercise yourself, start to finish.** Not skim it. Actually do it, on your own machine, with the same setup your participants will have. This is non-negotiable and it is where most bad sessions are lost. You will find the broken link, the outdated command, the step that assumes something nobody has.

**Write down three questions you cannot answer.** Every topic has them. Knowing yours in advance means that when someone asks, you say "great question, that is one I flagged as beyond me - let us look it up together" instead of improvising something wrong.

**Prepare one analogy for the hardest concept.** If you cannot explain it without jargon, you do not understand it well enough yet. Spend another hour.

**Know your first five minutes cold.** Openings set the tone. If you fumble the start, the room reads the whole session as shaky.

**Have a fallback.** WiFi dies. The demo breaks. Have something you can do on a whiteboard with no internet.

## Running the session

### Opening (5 minutes)

- What we are covering today, in one sentence
- Why it matters, in one sentence
- What you will be able to do by the end
- **Round the room:** name and one thing they want to get out of today. Even online. This single habit changes participation more than anything else, because someone who has spoken once will speak again.

### The body

**Talk for a maximum of 15 minutes before people do something.** Attention collapses after that. Break every block with an exercise, a question to the room, or a two-minute pair discussion.

**Ask more than you tell.** "What do you think happens if we reuse this address?" produces more learning than explaining it. Wait through the silence - count to seven in your head. People need longer to answer than feels comfortable, and the facilitator who fills the gap trains the room to stay quiet.

**Watch for the quiet ones.** In every room there is someone lost and too embarrassed to say so. Address it structurally rather than by singling them out: "Show of hands, who wants me to go over that again?" or "Let us take two minutes in pairs - explain it to each other."

**Let people struggle a little.** Do not jump in the moment someone hits an error. Struggle is where learning happens. Intervene at frustration, not at difficulty. Learning the difference is the craft.

### Closing (10 minutes)

- **What did we learn?** Ask the room, do not tell them. Their summary reveals what actually landed.
- **The contribution nudge.** Every session should end with a concrete next step toward open-source contribution - a good first issue to look at, a repo to clone, a doc to fix. This is what makes Code Orange different from a study group.
- **What is next session, and what to do before it.**

## When you do not know the answer

This will happen every session. How you handle it defines whether the room trusts you.

**Do this:**
> "I do not know. Let us find out."

Then actually find out, live. Open the BIP. Search the codebase. Ask in Discord. **Watching you find an answer is more valuable than hearing you recite one** - you are modelling the actual skill, which is knowing how to resolve your own confusion.

**Never do this:**
- Bluff. Someone in the room will know, and you lose the room permanently.
- Wave it off as unimportant.
- Promise to follow up and then not follow up.

**If nobody can resolve it in five minutes:** park it visibly. Keep a "parked questions" list, answer it before next session, and open with the answer. A facilitator who returns with answers builds enormous credibility.

## Common failure modes

| Problem | What is actually happening | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Nobody talks | The room does not feel safe, or you fill silences too fast | Round-the-room openers. Count to seven. Pair discussions before full-group. |
| One person dominates | Usually enthusiasm, not malice | "Let us hear from someone who has not spoken yet." Give them a job - helping a neighbour. |
| Huge skill spread | Normal, not a failure | Pair strong with struggling. Teaching is the best learning. Have a stretch task ready. |
| Everyone falls behind on setup | You did not test on their machines | Send setup instructions 48h ahead. Run a 15-min pre-session setup clinic. |
| Attendance decays after week 3 | Almost always cadence or difficulty cliff | Ask the people who left. Split the hard week into two. |
| Session runs long every time | Over-scoped | Cut 30% of your content. Every time. You will still run long. |

## Online versus in person

**Online:**
- Cameras encouraged, never mandatory. Bandwidth and privacy are real constraints.
- Use breakout rooms every 20 minutes. Passive listening online is worse than in person.
- Chat is a participation channel - watch it, read questions aloud, answer them.
- Record if everyone consents, but ask each session. Consent is not permanent.

**In person:**
- Test the projector before people arrive. Always.
- Power strips. Nothing kills a three-hour workshop faster than dying laptops.
- Seat people in pairs or small clusters, never lecture rows.
- Food, if you can. It changes the atmosphere entirely.
- Ask before photographing anyone. Every time.

## Making it stick

**Spacing beats intensity.** Six weekly 90-minute sessions produce more retention than one nine-hour day.

**Retrieval beats review.** Start each session asking what they remember, not by summarising. Pulling from memory is what builds it.

**Producing beats consuming.** A participant who writes a bad explanation learns more than one who reads a good one.

**Teaching beats everything.** Get participants explaining to each other as early as possible. The moment someone teaches a concept, they own it - and you have just found your next Apprentice Educator.

## The Code Orange difference

Plenty of people run Bitcoin study groups. What makes this program distinct is that **every session points at contribution.**

Not "someday you could contribute." This session, this week, here is a specific issue in a specific repo that relates to what we just learned.

Concretely:
- End every session with a real link to a real repo
- Celebrate first PRs loudly - in the room, in Discord, in your monthly report
- When someone gets a PR merged, have them tell the room how it went, including the review comments
- Track it. Every contribution from your sessions belongs in the [PR dashboard](https://github.com/code-orange-dev/PR-tracking-dashboard)

A study group creates people who understand Bitcoin. This program creates people who improve it. The gap between those two is entirely made of specific, actionable next steps - and providing them is your job.
